1. Challenge Track: What NASA Is Actually Asking
Official 2026 challenge: “Identify Earth Locations that Analog the Permanent Moon Base Locations and Mars.” NASA describes a real exploration workflow: future lunar/Martian landing and base decisions depend on terrain, geology, environment, resources, and other factors. Earth locations with analogous characteristics let mission teams test equipment and methods before flight, but existing terrestrial analogs are not fully characterized. The challenge asks teams to use open Earth, Moon, and Mars data to identify and characterize new terrestrial analogs and present the findings as a map, visualization, or other tool. [R1]
Plain-English challenge: “Before we send equipment or people to the Moon or Mars, where on Earth can we safely practice—and what, exactly, does that Earth location realistically simulate?”
NASA already conducts analog missions and planetary field research. That is not our invention. NASA uses terrestrial or engineered environments to test systems, procedures, science operations, crew behavior, and exploration technologies before using them in space. [R2][R3] The opportunity is therefore not to tell NASA that analog testing exists; it is to make analog selection and characterization more explicit, mission-specific, evidence-traceable, and actionable.
The challenge explicitly centers Earth + Moon + Mars data, so NASA/partner data must be the scientific foundation, not decoration.
The project must identify and characterize terrestrial analogs; a rover simulator alone would be off-track.
A strong extension is to connect an analog to the equipment or procedure it exists to test, because NASA itself describes analogs as a way to test systems and methods before flight. [R2][R3]
The challenge is approachable for judges: everyone can understand the idea of practicing on Earth before going to another world.
2. The Problem We Are Solving
The obvious interpretation of this challenge is: “find places on Earth that look like Mars or the Moon.” That is not enough. It naturally leads to a crowded class of solutions: similarity maps, ranking scores, 3D globes, and AI explanations.
Core insight: There is no universally “best Mars analog” or “best Moon analog.” There is only an analog that is useful for a specific experiment, mission operation, or piece of equipment.
A rocky volcanic field can be excellent for rover mobility and geology testing but poor for thermal testing. A polar site can be useful for cold operations but not for matching terrain. No natural Earth site reproduces lunar gravity or vacuum. Therefore, the meaningful question is not “How Mars-like is this place?” It is “What can this place validly test for this specific mission, what can it not test, and how do we prove that?”

3. Final Product: AnalogForge
AnalogForge is a mission-conditioned planetary analog qualification and test-design system. A mission team provides a Moon/Mars target, what it needs to test, and optionally a rover profile. AnalogForge uses NASA planetary and Earth data to discover candidate terrestrial analogs, qualify them requirement-by-requirement, identify conditions Earth cannot reproduce, generate rover-specific benchmarks, and run a lightweight “Prove It” test that can invalidate or refine the recommendation.
Final one-line pitch: “AnalogForge finds the right place on Earth to test a specific Moon or Mars mission, proves what that place can and cannot simulate using NASA data, and creates rover-specific benchmarks to verify the test.”
The project has three memorable product ideas. These should be the only three headline ideas in the pitch; the richer agent system lives underneath them.
Headline feature
What it means
Why judges remember it
1. Mission Fingerprint

2. Analog Qualification + Analog Gaps

3. PROVE IT

4. Why This Can Differentiate in a Crowded Track
A generic analog finder is the most obvious response to the prompt and therefore the most likely to be crowded. AnalogForge deliberately moves the differentiator from “discovery” to “qualification.” Discovery remains mandatory because the challenge asks for new analogs, but it is not our final value proposition.
The key transformation is:
Moon/Mars target → mission need → required evidence → candidate Earth sites → qualification → rover/site benchmarks → PROVE IT → PASS/FAIL → agent critique → re-plan → Analog Passport
This preserves near-zero prompt distance while creating high creative distance. The judge can map every major feature back to the original challenge: identify locations, characterize them, explain where the analogy breaks down, and show how the analog helps test equipment or methods.

6. End-to-End User Journey
1.  Choose a target world and region: Moon or Mars. The hackathon demo should use one well-prepared scenario rather than support every region.
2.  Describe the mission test in ordinary language: e.g., “Validate a rover that must cross rough 8–18° terrain and collect two geological samples.”
3.  Connect the rover: enter capabilities manually or upload a small structured rover profile.
4.  Mission Fingerprint appears: AnalogForge converts the request into measurable test dimensions and flags what cannot be reproduced naturally on Earth.
5.  Agent Council runs an investigation: planetary data specialist characterizes the target; Earth scout searches candidate areas; qualification agent measures coverage; critic challenges weak evidence.
6.  Earth candidate map appears: each site is shown with a requirement-by-requirement qualification rather than a magical overall “Mars score.”
7.  If no single location covers the mission, AnalogForge can propose a Composite Analog portfolio: one site for mobility/geology, another for thermal/operational exposure, and explicit residual gaps for simulation/facilities.
8.  User selects a site and clicks PROVE IT.
9.  Test Designer creates a rover-specific field benchmark using the selected terrain: required slope bands, roughness exposure, science waypoints, energy reserve, forbidden terrain, and success criteria.
10.  Verification engine runs the test. If it fails, the Verification/Replanning Agent diagnoses whether the issue is the route, the rover capability, or the analog itself, then re-plans.
11.  Analog Passport is generated: target, site, qualified uses, unsupported uses, NASA evidence, rover compatibility, test outcome, uncertainty, and recommended next step.

9. Analog Qualification Model and Analog Gaps
Avoid a single fake-precision score such as “Iceland is 92% Mars.” AnalogForge should report mission test coverage by dimension. Each dimension has: requirement, observable, data source, transformation/proxy, threshold or comparison rule, evidence strength, and uncertainty.
Qualification state
Meaning
PASS / Strong
Earth site provides the measurable condition needed for this mission test with adequate evidence.
PARTIAL
Site reproduces part of the condition or only a weak/limited proxy.
FAIL
Site does not provide enough of the required condition for the planned test.
GAP — Not naturally reproducible
No Earth location can directly reproduce the condition by site selection alone (e.g., lunar gravity, vacuum).
UNKNOWN
Relevant data is missing, incompatible in scale, or too uncertain; agent must seek more evidence or downgrade the claim.
Composite Analogs are optional but strategically valuable. The optimizer should ask: “What is the smallest set of Earth locations that collectively covers the largest fraction of mission-test requirements?” The product should penalize unnecessary sites so it does not recommend ten locations just to maximize coverage.
10. Agentic AI: The AnalogForge Agent Council
Agentic AI must be a functional operating layer, not a marketing label. The agents are responsible for deciding what to investigate, choosing approved tools/data, challenging conclusions, designing tests, and revising the plan. The agents are NOT allowed to invent scientific measurements. A single underlying model may implement multiple roles; the “multi-agent” design refers to separated responsibilities, prompts, tools, and checks rather than requiring many independent model deployments.
Agent
Responsibility
1. Mission Director / Orchestrator
Owns the overall objective. Creates the investigation plan, assigns tasks, tracks unresolved requirements, and decides when enough evidence exists to produce a recommendation.
2. Requirements Agent
Turns natural-language mission intent into a structured Mission Test Contract: objectives, constraints, testable dimensions, priority, and success criteria.
3. Rover Systems Agent
Reads the rover profile, identifies hard limits and capabilities, and converts them into rover-specific benchmark constraints.
4. Planetary Target Agent
Uses approved Moon/Mars tools to characterize the target environment: terrain, slopes/roughness, thermal context where available, and other supported properties.
5. Earth Analog Scout Agent
Searches candidate Earth regions using approved coarse filters, then asks the science tools to characterize only the most promising regions.
6. Qualification Agent
Builds the requirement-by-requirement qualification matrix and calculates mission-test coverage. It cannot override missing evidence with language-model intuition.
7. Scientific Critic Agent
Adversarial reviewer. Questions incompatible resolutions, weak proxies, missing evidence, overconfident claims, and whether an Earth condition truly answers the stated test question.
8. Test Designer Agent
Converts the qualified site + rover profile into the smallest useful verification experiment: route, required terrain exposures, waypoints, tasks, and success criteria.
9. Verification & Replanning Agent
Reads deterministic test results/telemetry. Diagnoses whether failure came from route, rover limits, test design, or an invalid analog assumption; then requests the smallest necessary re-plan.
10. Evidence / Passport Agent
Produces the final Analog Passport with provenance, assumptions, qualified uses, gaps, benchmark outcome, and a concise explanation suitable for judges.
The most important “committee” behavior is disagreement. Example: the Earth Scout proposes a candidate; the Qualification Agent marks terrain as strong; the Scientific Critic notices that the mineral layer is missing or the scale is incompatible; the Mission Director reopens the investigation instead of presenting a confident answer. This creates visible scientific skepticism rather than an AI echo chamber.
MISSION DIRECTOR  ├─ Requirements Agent → Mission Test Contract  ├─ Rover Systems Agent → Capability / hard limits  ├─ Planetary Target Agent → Target evidence  ├─ Earth Scout Agent → Candidate sites  ├─ Qualification Agent → PASS/PARTIAL/FAIL/GAP matrix  ├─ Scientific Critic → challenge / request more evidence  ├─ Test Designer → rover-specific benchmark  ├─ Verification/Replanner → execute → diagnose → revise  └─ Evidence Agent → Analog Passport
For judge-facing transparency, show a structured activity log such as: “Need target slope distribution → called LOLA/MOLA tool → computed slope histogram → need Earth candidate comparison → called NASADEM tool → critic flagged missing mineral evidence.” Do not expose hidden chain-of-thought; show operations, evidence, decisions, and results.

Avoid scientific overclaims. Earth and planetary terrain products differ in spatial resolution, sensing method, gravity, surface mechanics, coordinate system, and geology. 13. Full Live Example: Mars Rover Mobility + Geological Sampling
This example is illustrative and is meant to define the product behavior. Numbers are demo assumptions until computed from the final datasets.
Step
What the user sees / what AnalogForge does
1. Mission request
“Validate a future Mars rover that must autonomously cross rough terrain, traverse meaningful slopes, reach two exposed geology targets, and return with ≥20% battery reserve.”
2. Connect rover
Rover profile: 42 kg; max slope 18°; ground clearance 0.20 m; 1.8 kWh battery; ≥20% reserve; stereo + IMU + LiDAR.
3. Mission Fingerprint
Agent identifies terrain slope distribution, roughness/relief, route continuity, geology/mineral context, energy exposure, and non-Earth-reproducible factors such as Mars gravity/atmosphere.
4. Planetary characterization
Planetary Target Agent calls MOLA tools and creates the target terrain profile; stretch layer adds supported geology context.
5. Earth discovery
Earth Scout screens plausible arid/volcanic regions, then runs NASADEM + optional EMIT analysis on top candidates.
6. Qualification
Site A: terrain PASS; geology PASS/PARTIAL; thermal UNKNOWN; gravity GAP; atmosphere GAP. Site B has weaker terrain but better geology or another required property.
7. Rover compatibility
Rover Systems Agent checks that the chosen test zone can expose the rover to near-limit slopes without forcing prohibited terrain.
8. PROVE IT
Test Designer generates a route requiring defined distance across 8–12°, 12–15°, and 15–18° slope bins plus two science waypoints and an energy reserve.
9. Failure
Deterministic test returns FAIL: candidate zone does not contain enough contiguous 15–18° terrain, or modeled energy reserve drops below 20%.
10. Replan
Verification Agent diagnoses whether the issue is route, rover capability, or site qualification. It can move the test zone, revise route, or reject Site A and investigate Site B.
11. Final Passport
“Qualified for rover mobility + field sampling under these assumptions; not qualified for Mars gravity/atmosphere; route benchmark PASSED after re-plan; evidence and data provenance attached.”
Judge takeaway: “A place can look Mars-like and still be useless for the actual test. AnalogForge checks the test, not just the resemblance.”

15. Global-Level Expansion Without Changing the Product
If the team becomes a Global Nominee, the product identity should remain the same. Add scientific depth rather than adding unrelated features.
Expand Earth search beyond a small curated candidate set with coarse-to-fine screening.
Add one additional scientifically useful layer: EMIT mineralogy, Diviner temperature, or another official challenge resource.
Benchmark against several known NASA analogs from the Analog Explorer as positive controls and obvious non-analogs as negative controls.
Add uncertainty/sensitivity analysis: show how a recommendation changes when mission priorities or data resolution change.
Implement Composite Analog optimization formally and quantify coverage without fake planetary similarity percentages.
Allow more rover/test profiles and possibly accept a simple URDF/ROS configuration as a stretch input—still no arbitrary low-level hardware code generation.
Produce stronger provenance: source product, acquisition/resolution, derived calculation, assumptions, and agent decision for every major claim.

20. Explicit Non-Goals
These constraints protect the project from becoming an impressive but unfocused robotics demo.
No claim that NASA currently lacks analog testing or expert analog selection.
No single “% Mars-like” score presented as scientific truth.
No full rocket, spacecraft, rover, propulsion, structural, or flight-control design.
No autonomous generation/deployment of safety-critical motor or flight firmware.
No requirement for a physical rover to make the project convincing.
No full CAD ingestion in the local MVP; manual/JSON rover specs are enough.
No photorealistic digital twin requirement; a meaningful terrain/route verification is more important.
No 20-agent swarm for appearance. Agent roles must map to a real decision or verification responsibility.
No forced use of sponsor tools just to list sponsor names; NASA data remains central.
No unsupported claim that an Earth site reproduces lunar/Martian gravity, vacuum, radiation, or regolith mechanics.

24. Final Scope Lock
BUILD THIS: AnalogForge is an agentic, mission-conditioned Earth analog qualification tool. It takes a Moon/Mars mission objective + rover profile, uses NASA planetary/Earth data to find candidate Earth sites, qualifies each site against the exact mission test, exposes Analog Gaps, generates rover-specific pass/fail benchmarks, executes one lightweight PROVE IT test, and re-plans if the evidence or test fails.
For the local hackathon, success is one polished golden-path scenario with real NASA terrain data, one agent critic loop, one rover benchmark, one real computed failure/re-plan, and one excellent Analog Passport. Everything else is secondary.
